# Lesson 1: Understanding Ratios
**Unit:** Ratios and Proportional Relationships | **Grade:** 6 | **Duration:** 60 minutes

**Standard(s):** 6.RP.1 – Understand the concept of a ratio and use ratio language

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:

### BASIC
- Identify what a ratio is and describe it as a comparison of two quantities
- Write a simple ratio in at least one form (e.g., 3:1)

### INTERMEDIATE
- Express ratios in three different forms (3:1, "3 to 1", 3/1)
- Identify ratios in real-world contexts and describe the relationship clearly

### ADVANCED
- Create and explain ratios in unfamiliar contexts
- Analyze why order matters in ratios and justify why 3:1 ≠ 1:3

## Detailed Lesson Plan

### PHASE 1: Hook/Warm-Up (10 minutes)

**Objective:** Activate prior thinking and build interest

**Activity: "What Do You Notice?"**

Display this image or scenario:
> *A pizza shop has created a special pizza. It has 3 slices covered in pepperoni and 1 slice that's just cheese.*

**Ask students:**
- "What do you notice about the pizza slices?"
- "How many pepperoni slices are there?"
- "How many cheese slices are there?"
- "How could we describe this? Is it the same thing as saying 'we have 4 slices'?"

**Expected responses:**
- "There are more pepperoni than cheese"
- "3 and 1"
- "Three times as many pepperoni"

**Transition statement:**
"Today we're learning about a way to compare quantities. Mathematicians call this a **RATIO**. A ratio compares two things, just like we're comparing pepperoni slices to cheese slices."

**Why this hook works:**
- Relatable context (pizza, food)
- Visual (students can see the comparison)
- Builds on intuitive understanding before formal definition

---

### PHASE 2: Direct Instruction (15 minutes)

**Objective:** Explicitly teach what a ratio is and how to write it

#### Part A: Define a Ratio (5 minutes)

**Write on board/chart:**
```
RATIO = A comparison of two quantities
```

**Say:**
"A ratio tells us the relationship between two amounts. When we compare the pepperoni slices to the cheese slices, we're creating a ratio."

**Show examples on chart paper:**

```
EXAMPLE 1: Pizza Slices
Pepperoni slices: 3
Cheese slices: 1
RATIO: We compare 3 to 1

EXAMPLE 2: Your Classroom
Boys: 12
Girls: 13
RATIO: We compare 12 to 13
```

**Ask students:**
"In our pizza example, are we comparing exactly the same things? No! We're comparing a **part** (pepperoni) to another **part** (cheese). This is called a **part-to-part ratio**."

---

#### Part B: Three Ways to Write a Ratio (7 minutes)

**Show the pizza ratio with three notations:**

| Notation | Written | Read Aloud |
|----------|---------|-----------|
| **Colon** | 3:1 | "3 to 1" |
| **Words** | 3 to 1 | "3 to 1" |
| **Fraction** | 3/1 | "3 over 1" (or "3 to 1") |

**Write all three on chart and have students repeat:**
- "Three colon one" (point to 3:1)
- "Three to one" (point to 3 to 1)
- "Three over one" (point to 3/1)

**Key teaching point:**
"These all mean the **same ratio**. It's like saying 3:1, 3 to 1, and 3/1 are different languages for the same idea. They all compare 3 of something to 1 of something else."

---

#### Part C: Order Matters! (3 minutes)

**This is critical—address the misconception directly:**

**Draw on chart:**
```
3:1  is NOT the same as  1:3

3:1 means: 3 pepperoni slices to 1 cheese slice
1:3 means: 1 pepperoni slice to 3 cheese slices

These are DIFFERENT!
```

**Use manipulatives:**
- Show 3 red tiles and 1 blue tile (for 3:1)
- Then flip it: show 1 red tile and 3 blue tiles (for 1:3)
- Ask: "Are these the same? No! Order matters in a ratio!"

**Emphasize:**
"Just like 3 and 1 are different, 3:1 and 1:3 are different. We must be careful about which quantity comes first."

---

### PHASE 3: Guided Practice (20 minutes)

**Objective:** Practice identifying and writing ratios with teacher support

#### Activity 1: Classroom Ratio Exploration (10 minutes)

**What you do:**
1. Have students look around the classroom
2. Point out different groupings:
   - "Count boys. Count girls."
   - "Count windows. Count doors."
   - "Count desks. Count students."

**Students do (with your guidance):**
- Suggest: "Let's count boys first... I see about 12 boys. Now let's count girls... I see about 13 girls. What's our ratio?"
- Write together on chart: **12:13** and **12 to 13**
- Ask: "Does 13:12 mean the same thing? Why or why not?"

**Do this 3-4 times with different attributes:**
- Boys:Girls (part-to-part)
- Windows:Doors (part-to-part)
- Desks:Students (part-to-whole, if number of desks ≠ students)

**Why this works:**
- Students are measuring their own classroom
- Teacher models thinking process
- Class creates shared examples

---

#### Activity 2: Ratio Cards (10 minutes)

**Setup:**
Hand out or show picture cards with objects in different quantities.

**Examples:**
- Picture 1: 5 apples and 2 oranges
- Picture 2: 3 cats and 4 dogs
- Picture 3: 7 circles and 7 squares

**Guided practice steps:**

1. **Show Picture 1 (apples & oranges)**
   - "Count the apples. How many?" → 5
   - "Count the oranges. How many?" → 2
   - "What's our ratio?" → 5:2
   - "Write this in words." → 5 to 2
   - "What does 5:2 tell us?" → There are 5 apples for every 2 oranges

2. **Show Picture 2 (cats & dogs)**
   - Repeat process; let students do more independently
   - Ask: "Is the ratio 3:4 or 4:3? How do you know?"

3. **Show Picture 3 (circles & squares)**
   - Ask: "What's special about this ratio?" → They're equal
   - "If I write 7:7, what does that mean?" → Same number of each

**Scaffold as needed:**
- Struggling: Use small numbers, provide sentence frame: "The ratio is ___ to ___"
- On-level: Use above examples
- Advanced: Ask to find multiple ratios in same picture (ratio of circles to total shapes, etc.)

### PHASE 4: Independent Practice (12 minutes)

**Objective:** Apply learning without teacher support

**Activity: Ratio Hunt Worksheet**

**Provide worksheet with 5-6 scenarios:**

```
RATIO HUNT
Name: ________________________  Date: ____________

1. Your classroom has 15 windows and 3 doors.
   Write the ratio of windows to doors.
   ___________ (colon form)
   ___________ (word form)

2. A baker made 12 chocolate cookies and 8 vanilla cookies.
   Write the ratio of chocolate cookies to vanilla cookies.
   ___________ (colon form)
   ___________ (word form)

3. In a school parking lot, there are 6 cars and 4 bikes.
   Write the ratio of cars to bikes.
   ___________:___________
   ___________ to ___________

4. A lemonade stand sold 20 small cups and 5 large cups.
   Write the ratio of small cups to large cups in ALL THREE forms.
   Colon: ___________
   Words: ___________ to ___________
   Fraction: ___________

5. Challenge: If the ratio of red beads to blue beads is 4:3,
   how many of each could you have? Draw a picture to show.
   Red: _____  Blue: _____
```

**Differentiation within independent work:**

**For struggling learners:**
- Provide only problems 1-3 (easier scenarios)
- Offer picture support or manipulatives to show
- Allow to work with a partner

**For on-level learners:**
- Complete all 5 problems as written
- May skip challenge if time needed

**For advanced learners:**
- Start with challenge problem first
- Add: "Write 3 different ratios from your own classroom" OR
- "If one ratio is 4:3, what does 3:4 tell you? Are they the same?"

## Common Misconceptions & How to Address

| Misconception | What students think | What to say/show |
|---|---|---|
| **Confusing ratio with fraction** | "3:1 is the same as 3/1" | "In a ratio, we compare two things. 3:1 says 3 of something for every 1 of something else. In a fraction, 3/1 = 3. They're different ideas." |
| **Order doesn't matter** | "3:1 and 1:3 are the same" | Show with tiles: 3 red, 1 blue vs. 1 red, 3 blue. "Look—these are different! Order tells us which quantity comes first." |
| **Ratio is just counting** | "3:1 means we have 3 and 1" | "Yes, we have 3 and 1, but we're comparing them. 3:1 tells us the relationship—we have 3 times as many of the first as the second." |
| **Simplification required** | "3:1 should be simplified like 3/1" | "Ratios don't simplify like fractions. 3:1 is already in the form we want." |

## Tips for Success

✅ **Use concrete objects first** – Tiles, counters, pictures help students see the comparison

✅ **Repeat the definition** – Say "ratio" many times; make it a familiar word

✅ **Use consistent contexts** – Stick with pizza/classroom/store examples to build understanding

✅ **Involve all students** – Use think-pair-share; get everyone talking about ratios

✅ **Watch for order confusion** – This is THE big misconception; address directly and repeatedly

✅ **Have fun with it** – Use humor, real contexts, student experiences
