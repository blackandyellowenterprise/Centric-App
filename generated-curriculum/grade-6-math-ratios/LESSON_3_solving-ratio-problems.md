# Lesson 3: Solving Ratio and Rate Problems
**Unit:** Ratios and Proportional Relationships | **Grade:** 6 | **Duration:** 60 minutes

**Standard(s):** 6.RP.3 – Use ratio and rate reasoning to solve real-world and mathematical problems

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:

### BASIC
- Solve simple one-step ratio problems
- Use one strategy (table, diagram, or equation) to find missing quantities

### INTERMEDIATE
- Solve multi-step ratio problems with real-world context
- Select and justify an appropriate strategy for given problem
- Use tables, tape diagrams, or number lines to solve problems

### ADVANCED
- Solve complex ratio problems using multiple representations
- Explain why different strategies yield the same answer
- Create word problems for given ratio solutions

## Detailed Lesson Plan

### PHASE 1: Hook/Warm-Up (8 minutes)

**Objective:** Introduce multi-step ratio problem

**Scenario:**

"You're planning a party. A recipe makes punch for 4 people and uses:
- 2 cups orange juice
- 3 cups ginger ale
- 1 cup lime juice

**But we have 12 people coming! How much of each ingredient do we need?**"

**Ask students (think-pair-share):**
- "How many times bigger is 12 than 4?" → 3 times
- "So how much orange juice do we need?" → Let them think
- "How would you figure this out?"

**Possible responses:**
- "Multiply each by 3"
- "Make a table"
- "Use ratios"

**Transition:**
"Today you're going to learn **multiple ways** to solve ratio problems. Different strategies work for different problems, and you'll pick which one makes the most sense."

---

### PHASE 2: Direct Instruction (18 minutes)

**Objective:** Teach three main strategies for solving ratio problems

---

#### Strategy 1: Using a Table of Equivalent Ratios (6 minutes)

**Show the punch problem:**

```
Original recipe (4 people):
- 2 cups orange juice
- 3 cups ginger ale
- 1 cup lime juice

For 12 people?
```

**Create a table on chart paper:**

| People | Orange Juice | Ginger Ale | Lime Juice |
|--------|--------------|-----------|-----------|
| 4      | 2 cups       | 3 cups    | 1 cup     |
| 8      | 4 cups       | 6 cups    | 2 cups    |
| 12     | **6 cups**   | **9 cups**| **3 cups**|

**Explain:**
"I made a table to show equivalent ratios. When I double the people (4→8), I double the ingredients (2→4). When I triple the people (4→12), I triple the ingredients (2→6)."

**Key idea:**
"The table helps us see the pattern: multiply both parts of the ratio by the same number."

**Show pattern:**
```
4 people → 2 cups OJ
4 × 3 = 12 people → 2 × 3 = 6 cups OJ
```

---

#### Strategy 2: Using a Tape Diagram (6 minutes)

**Same problem, different visual:**

Draw on chart paper:

```
RECIPE FOR 4 PEOPLE (1 unit):

Orange Juice:  [2 cups]
               4 people  (1 unit)

Ginger Ale:    [3 cups]
               4 people  (1 unit)

FOR 12 PEOPLE (3 units):

Orange Juice:  [2][2][2] = 2+2+2 = 6 cups

Ginger Ale:    [3][3][3] = 3+3+3 = 9 cups
```

**Explain:**
"A tape diagram shows the ratio as a bar. If 4 people need 2 cups, then 12 people need 3 bars of that same amount, so 2+2+2=6 cups."

**Key idea:**
"The tape diagram helps us **see** the multiplication. We're repeating the ratio 3 times."

---

#### Strategy 3: Using a Double Number Line (6 minutes)

Draw on chart paper:

```
PEOPLE:    0    4    8    12
            |____|____|____|

OJ (cups): 0    2    4    6

How it works:
- Bottom line shows people
- Top line shows cups of OJ
- Lines match up to show the ratio
- 4 people → 2 cups, 8 people → 4 cups, 12 people → 6 cups
```

**Explain:**
"A number line shows the same ratio in two ways. The spacing is equal – as we move along people, we move along oranges juice proportionally."

**Key idea:**
"A number line works great for rates (like miles per hour) or when you want to see the pattern."

---

**Summary of strategies:**

| Strategy | Best For | Example |
|----------|----------|---------|
| **Table** | Comparing many equivalent ratios | Recipe scaling, currency conversion |
| **Tape Diagram** | Seeing the repeating parts | "If 4 people need X, how many for 12?" |
| **Number Line** | Rates and continuous quantities | Speed, distance, time problems |

**Important:**
"All three strategies give the **same answer**. You pick the one that makes the most sense to you for that problem."

### PHASE 3: Guided Practice (22 minutes)

**Objective:** Apply different strategies with teacher support

#### Problem 1: Map Scale (7 minutes)

**Scenario:**
"On a map, 2 inches = 10 miles. If two cities are 8 inches apart on the map, how many miles apart are they in real life?"

**Teacher models Strategy 1 (Table):**
```
Map inches  |  Real miles
    2       |    10
    4       |    20
    6       |    30
    8       |    40
```

"I doubled, then doubled again, then added one more set. 8 inches = 40 miles."

**Ask students:**
- "Could we use a tape diagram? How?"
- "Could we use a number line? How?"

Let them sketch both strategies. (All should show 40 miles.)

---

#### Problem 2: Running Speed (7 minutes)

**Scenario:**
"Maya ran 15 miles in 3 hours. At this rate, how far will she run in 9 hours?"

**Students try Strategy 2 (Tape Diagram) with teacher support:**

Guide them:
- "What's our ratio? 15 miles in 3 hours"
- "How many hours in the new problem? 9 hours"
- "How many 3-hour blocks fit in 9 hours? 3 blocks"
- "Draw 3 blocks of '15 miles' each"
- "Add them up: 15 + 15 + 15 = ?"

**Answer:** 45 miles

---

#### Problem 3: Recipe Scaling (8 minutes)

**Scenario:**
"A smoothie recipe uses 6 strawberries for every 2 cups yogurt. If you use 5 cups yogurt, how many strawberries do you need?"

**Students choose their strategy with teacher circulation:**

**Struggling approach (teacher guides):**
- "Let's make a table"
- Strawberries: 6, 12, 18, ?
- Yogurt: 2, 4, 6, ... wait, we need 5
- Teacher: "Hmm, 5 isn't in our pattern. Let's think differently"
- "What's the ratio for 1 cup yogurt? 6 strawberries ÷ 2 = 3 strawberries per cup"
- "So 5 cups × 3 = 15 strawberries"

**On-level approach:**
- "Let me try a tape diagram"
- Recognize: 2 cups = 6 strawberries, so 4 cups = 12 strawberries
- Remaining: 1 cup = 3 strawberries
- Total: 12 + 3 = 15 strawberries

**Advanced approach:**
- "I'll use a double number line or unit rate"
- Unit rate: 3 strawberries per cup
- 5 cups × 3 = 15 strawberries

## Common Misconceptions & How to Address

| Misconception | What students think | What to say/show |
|---|---|---|
| **Adding instead of multiplying** | "If 4→12, I add 8, so 2→10" | "No, we multiply! 4 × 3 = 12, so 2 × 3 = 6. We're scaling up, not adding." |
| **Strategy confusion** | "I don't know which way to start" | "Pick the strategy that feels easiest for you. Table? Diagram? Number line? All work!" |
| **Forgetting to check** | Answers that are unreasonable | "Does your answer make sense? Check: Is 100 miles in 1 hour realistic? No!" |
| **Incomplete scaling** | "12 = 4 × 3, but I only multiplied one ingredient" | "Remember: multiply BOTH parts of the ratio by the same number" |

---

## Tips for Success

✅ **Let students choose their strategy** – Ownership increases engagement and understanding

✅ **Model all three publicly** – Show that different paths lead to same answer

✅ **Emphasize "multiply by the same number"** – This is the key insight

✅ **Use real problems students care about** – Food, games, money, sports work well

✅ **Have students explain their thinking** – Not just the answer, but WHY

✅ **Celebrate different approaches** – "I love how Emma used a table and Marcus used a number line. Both right!"

Cookies: 24, 48

[24 cookies, 2 cups] × 2 = [48 cookies, 4 cups]
Answer: 4 cups
